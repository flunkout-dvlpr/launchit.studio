A framework for early-stage ideas

Building got cheap. Validation didn't.

A working prototype used to take months and real money. Now it takes a weekend. That doesn't make an idea safer, it just moves the real risk somewhere less visible: whether the problem is real enough, for enough people, that they come back without being asked.

This page is a way to tell the difference between a conversation that feels like proof and a test that actually is one.

Three answers that aren't evidence

These come up in almost every one of these conversations, and all three feel like validation while costing the person saying them nothing.

Oh yeah, this is a real need I know the industry, this is much needed My friends, family and peers agree this is a real need

An opinion, about a hypothetical, from someone who isn't the customer and pays no cost for being wrong. None of them climb.

0 Opinion Anyone's view that the problem is real, including your own. Costs nothing to say, costs nothing to agree with. Dropbox is usually cited here too: a demo video and an overnight waitlist spike. It measured attention, not a decision to pay or change behavior, so it's weaker evidence than it's given credit for.
1 Problem interview A structured conversation with a real target customer about what already happened to them, not about your idea. Confirms the problem exists, how often, how painful, and what they already do about it today.
2 Costly commitment The person gives up something real, money, time or reputation, before the product fully exists. Airbnb rented air mattresses in their own apartment during a sold-out conference and took real payment, before any platform existed. DoorDash ran a one-page site listing a few restaurant menus and drove the food themselves, before any delivery software existed. Neither waited for permission to find out.
3 Recurring behavior People come back, pay again, or refer someone else, without you asking them to. Uber started in one city with one vehicle class, aimed at people already paying for black cars, and watched for repeat use before expanding to new cities or cheaper tiers.

Where a fresh prototype usually sits

A weekend build, demoed once, with the team and a few outside people agreeing it's useful, sits at tier 0 to 1. That's normal for a few days of work, and it's exactly why the next step is obvious once it's named:

  • 8 to 10 problem interviews with people who match the target profile but aren't already in your network, about what they did last time, before the idea is mentioned
  • One real, costly commitment: a waitlist with a deposit, a manual concierge version someone actually uses, a pre-order
  • A way to actually observe behavior, not just collect opinions about it, once real people are using it

Before building more, climb one more rung.

Built to pull up mid-conversation, not to read start to finish. The ladder stays the same from one idea to the next, only the box near the bottom changes.
